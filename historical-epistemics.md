# Historical Epistemics

This rulebook governs how to think about evidence, disagreement, and causal claims in political history.

---

## 1. Source tiers and use cases

Use sources in tiers, and label the tier when you use them.

- Tier 1: peer-reviewed scholarship, university-press monographs, and replicable datasets such as V-Dem, Polity, Correlates of War, Maddison, and World Bank data.
- Tier 2: well-edited scholarly synthesis, research institutes, and major academic publishers.
- Tier 3: think-tank work and popular journalism — orientation only, and must be labeled as such.

When a source is a popular grand narrative, do not present it as settled truth. Present it as a position, and include a professional critique alongside it.

---

## 2. Fact, interpretation, projection

Every page must keep these visibly distinct:
- Fact: an observed or documented claim that can be checked against data or records.
- Interpretation: a claim about what the fact means, why it mattered, or how it should be explained.
- Projection: a claim about the future, likely outcomes, or what would happen under a counterfactual.

A page should never collapse these into one undifferentiated paragraph.

---

## 3. Primary, secondary, and tertiary sources

- Primary sources are the documents or data produced by the actors, institutions, or contemporaries in question.
- Secondary sources are scholarly or historical interpretations of those documents and events.
- Tertiary sources are syntheses and overviews that are useful for orientation but not sufficient on their own.

For premodern history, the surviving record is usually written by the winners, the literate elite, or a later dynasty with reasons to indict the last ruler. This makes source selection and caution especially important.

---

## 4. How to write about schools of interpretation

Do not feign a view from nowhere. Name the school inline when discussing a claim:
- realist explanation
- institutionalist explanation
- world-systems explanation
- constructivist explanation

Use the school label as a cue to the mechanism and the evidence burden, not as a disguise for a mere assertion.

---

## 5. Popular grand narratives

Popular authors such as Diamond, Harari, Turchin, Ferguson, and Acemoglu & Robinson are often useful as statements of a position and as entry points into a debate. But each must be paired with a substantial professional critique. A page should say what the author contributes and why specialists reject or qualify it.

---

## 6. Methods to use from Unit 1 onward

The following methods are not saved for the end:
- comparative method
- case selection and its traps
- disciplined counterfactual reasoning
- causal inference in observational settings
- forecasting

The system should treat these as everyday habits, not as a final capstone chapter.

---

## 7. How to handle contested causal claims

For any serious disagreement, build a debate page rather than smoothing over the dispute. The page should include:
- the question stated precisely
- the strongest version of each major position
- the best objection to each
- what evidence would discriminate between them
- a section on what is settled vs. what is interpretation

---

## 8. The anti-hindsight rule

History is full of false inevitability. Never present an outcome as if it were obvious from the conditions before the outcome was known. A good page should make the uncertainty visible, not erase it.
