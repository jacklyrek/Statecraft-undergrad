# The Science of Learning Fast: A Working Reference for Self-Study

*Adapted for statecraft study: historical knowledge is contested, retrieval must be structured against hindsight and presentism, and the most valuable prompts are decision and counterfactual exercises.*

---

## How to use this document

This is a reference for building the study system, not a manifesto. The central idea is simple: the act of trying to retrieve knowledge from memory is itself the thing that builds the memory. The techniques below are the ones that make that happen reliably.

The most useful high-utility methods are still:
- retrieval practice
- spaced repetition
- interleaving
- feedback

The new discipline for this subject is to make the retrieval effort resistant to the common traps of history: hindsight bias, narrative fallacy, presentism, and anachronism.

---

## Part 1 — What the research actually says

### Retrieval practice

Testing yourself is not merely measurement. The retrieval attempt changes memory. This is one of the most replicated findings in cognitive psychology.

The practical lesson for this system:
- prefer recall, cloze, explain, and decision prompts over passive review
- reveal the answer after the attempt, not before
- keep the feedback loop explicit

### Spaced repetition

The same material should be revisited across multiple sessions separated in time. The scheduler should not let the same item dominate a single sitting or vanish for months.

### Interleaving

Interleave confusable material: realism vs. liberalism vs. constructivism; tax farming vs. salaried collection; presidential vs. parliamentary systems; collapse cases that look similar at first glance.

### The anti-hindsight layer

History is unusually vulnerable to the fluency illusion and hindsight bias. Items should be designed so they do not accidentally leak the outcome into the prompt. A prediction prompt should state the conditions but not the result.

The following failure modes should be built into the item design:
- hindsight bias: outcomes look inevitable after the fact
- narrative fallacy: good stories are not evidence
- survivorship / selection on the dependent variable: studying only famous successes or collapses distorts the comparison
- great-man attribution: leaders are not the whole story
- presentism: the past is not the present with different clothes
- anachronism: modern mechanisms should not be assumed to have existed in earlier eras

Items should regularly ask for the strongest objection, the evidence that would change your mind, and the difference between a fact, an interpretation, and a projection.

## Part 2 — Turning the research into tools

### Item types

The system should be built around these item types:
- `cloze` for definitions and stable facts
- `recall` for short free recall
- `mcq` for discrimination tasks
- `compare` for confusables
- `explain` for causal reasoning and mechanism
- `case` for reading a historical scenario and identifying what it is and is not good evidence for
- `decision` for ruler's-chair prompts
- `counterfactual` for testing what a theory predicts under a removed cause
- `attribution` for explaining an event through competing frameworks
- `forecast` for predicting an outcome before seeing it

Every item should carry a clear prompt, answer, topic, cluster, and source_page. When the answer depends on era, add `era`.

### Bloom coverage

Do not ship only remember-level items. For each major concept, the bank should include:
- remember
- understand
- apply
- analyze
- evaluate

The decisive items for this subject are often `analyze` and `evaluate` prompts: compare institutions, judge the strength of a causal claim, or identify the strongest objection.

### Interleaving and clustering

Cluster confusable concepts and make items that compare them directly. Examples:
- realism vs. liberalism vs. constructivism
- tax farming vs. salaried bureaucracy
- presidential vs. parliamentary vs. semi-presidential systems
- Rome vs. Qing vs. Ottoman vs. USSR collapse

### The ruler's-eye stance

The system should repeatedly test the middle ground between inevitability and heroic agency. Some choices matter. Some do not. The hard part of rule is usually revenue, coalitions, military obedience, information distortion, and legitimacy under stress.

## Part 3 — Applying this to statecraft

For statecraft, the most useful items are not just factual but decision-like:
- decision prompts: what do you do in the chair?
- counterfactual prompts: what changes if X is removed?
- attribution prompts: how would different frameworks explain this event?
- forecast prompts: what does a framework predict before the outcome is known?

These are more valuable than rote definitions because they train the same reasoning a ruler actually needs.
