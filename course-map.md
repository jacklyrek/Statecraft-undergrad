# Course Map — Statecraft

This is the research and planning brief for the statecraft spine. It is designed to support an undergraduate-level course in political order, historical state formation, and practical rule. Each unit lists its core intellectual problem, its major learning objectives, its dependencies, the conceptual clusters it develops, and the eras and cases that will anchor the material.

---

## Tier 1 — Taking and Keeping Power

### Unit 1 — What You Are Actually Seizing
- **Core problem:** How does a ruler move from formal authority to actual control over the state?
- **Objectives:** distinguish state, regime, government, and political order; analyze sovereignty, monopoly on violence, state formation, and state capacity; explain why state formation is an organizational and coercive problem as much as a legal one.
- **Depends on:** —
- **Clusters:** state-formation, sovereignty-vs-government, coercion-and-extraction, capacity-vs-fragility
- **Eras:** chiefdom, agrarian empire, composite monarchy, industrial nation-state, contemporary state
- **Cases:** Achaemenid Persia, Rome, Han China, early modern Europe, the modern state
- **Why this matters:** This unit establishes the baseline for every later unit. If a ruler cannot secure coercion, extraction, and coordination, everything else is unstable.

### Unit 2 — Who Actually Keeps You in Power
- **Core problem:** Why do rulers depend on coalitions, and how do they keep them loyal?
- **Objectives:** analyze patronage, brokerage, elite bargaining, coalition maintenance, and the logic of reward distribution; explain how coalition size changes incentives and governance strategy.
- **Depends on:** Unit 1
- **Clusters:** patronage-vs-public-goods, selectorate-vs-winning-coalitions, loyalty-and-defection
- **Eras:** all eras
- **Cases:** Rome, the Abbasid caliphate, the Mongol succession crisis, early modern dynasties
- **Why this matters:** Many regimes fail not because they lack authority, but because they fail to sustain the coalition that made power possible.

### Unit 3 — Legitimacy and Succession
- **Core problem:** How do rulers make obedience seem rightful, and how do regimes survive the transfer of power?
- **Objectives:** distinguish legitimacy from fear or mere compliance; analyze ritual, symbolism, legal claim-making, and institutional continuity; evaluate succession as a regime-level vulnerability.
- **Depends on:** Unit 2
- **Clusters:** legitimacy-types, ritual-and-performance, succession-crises
- **Eras:** all eras
- **Cases:** Han China, the Ottoman Empire, the USSR, dynastic transitions in Europe and Asia
- **Why this matters:** Legitimacy stabilizes rule; succession tests whether the regime can reproduce itself without total breakdown.

### Unit 4 — The Means of Coercion
- **Core problem:** How do rulers organize force without creating a rival center of power?
- **Objectives:** analyze military organization, police systems, mercenaries, standing armies, and the coup-proofing problem; explain how military design shapes regime durability.
- **Depends on:** Unit 2, Unit 3
- **Clusters:** coup-proofing, military-form, coercion-and-politics
- **Eras:** all eras
- **Cases:** Rome, Venice, the Ottoman Empire, the modern military state
- **Why this matters:** Coercion is both a tool of rule and a permanent political risk.

## Tier 2 — Making the Machine Work

### Unit 5 — Money and Fiscal Capacity
- **Core problem:** How do rulers raise revenue and make the state financially viable?
- **Objectives:** analyze taxation, debt, inflation, tribute, land revenue, customs, and fiscal fragility; explain how fiscal institutions shape war-making and state capacity.
- **Depends on:** Unit 1
- **Clusters:** taxation-types, fiscal-military-state, sovereign-finance
- **Eras:** agrarian empire, early-modern monarchy, industrial state, contemporary state
- **Cases:** Spain's silver century, the Qing Empire, the Ottoman fiscal system, the USSR
- **Why this matters:** Fiscal capacity is the material foundation of administrative and military power.

### Unit 6 — Administration and Information Distortion
- **Core problem:** How do rulers make decisions travel through complex organizations without becoming distorted?
- **Objectives:** analyze bureaucracy, legibility, administrative reach, principal-agent problems, corruption, and information asymmetry; explain what technological change has changed about the problem of distance.
- **Depends on:** Unit 5
- **Clusters:** bureaucracy-vs-corruption, legibility-vs-distortion, implementation-and-resistance
- **Eras:** agrarian empire, industrial state, contemporary state
- **Cases:** Qing China, Meiji Japan, the PRC reform era, modern bureaucracies
- **Why this matters:** The state often fails not because the policy is wrong but because implementation breaks down in transit.

### Unit 7 — Binding Yourself
- **Core problem:** How do rulers create institutions that make rule more predictable and credible?
- **Objectives:** analyze rule of law, credible commitment, constitutions, courts, property rights, and public institutions; explain how institutions help rulers borrow, survive, and govern over time.
- **Depends on:** Unit 5, Unit 6
- **Clusters:** rule-of-law-vs-personal-rule, constitutional-forms, credible-commitment
- **Eras:** early-modern monarchy, industrial state, contemporary state
- **Cases:** England, France, Singapore, modern constitutional states
- **Why this matters:** Institutions can make rulers more durable, but only if they are enforced and politically meaningful.

### Unit 8 — Deciding Under Pressure
- **Core problem:** How do rulers make decisions under uncertainty, crisis, and organizational complexity?
- **Objectives:** analyze advisory systems, bureaucracy, bounded rationality, groupthink, and crisis decision-making; evaluate how much leaders truly control events.
- **Depends on:** Unit 1, Unit 6
- **Clusters:** crisis-decision-models, structure-vs-agency, bounded-rationality
- **Eras:** all eras
- **Cases:** Weimar, the USSR, Britain, wartime states
- **Why this matters:** Governance is often less about grand plans than about making good decisions under bad information.

## Tier 3 — Surviving History

### Unit 9 — Neighbors and Grand Strategy
- **Core problem:** How do domestic constraints shape foreign policy and war-making?
- **Objectives:** analyze anarchy, the security dilemma, alliances, deterrence, hegemony, trade, and war as a political instrument; compare realist, liberal, and constructivist perspectives.
- **Depends on:** Unit 4, Unit 5
- **Clusters:** realism-vs-liberalism-vs-constructivism, deterrence, grand-strategy
- **Eras:** all eras
- **Cases:** Britain, the Habsburgs, the PRC, imperial rivalries
- **Why this matters:** Foreign policy is shaped by the same coalition, fiscal, and legitimacy constraints that structure domestic politics.

### Unit 10 — Rupture from Within
- **Core problem:** Why do states break down from within, and how do rulers respond?
- **Objectives:** analyze revolution, civil war, insurgency, democratization, and authoritarian resilience; connect legitimacy, inequality, repression, and social mobilization to breakdown.
- **Depends on:** Unit 3, Unit 9
- **Clusters:** revolution-vs-civil-war, democratization-vs-backsliding, mass-mobilization
- **Eras:** industrial state, contemporary state
- **Cases:** France, Venezuela, Argentina, modern civil wars
- **Why this matters:** Internal rupture is often the collapse of the coalitions and legitimacy that made rule possible.

### Unit 11 — Prosperity and the Resource Base
- **Core problem:** What conditions make states prosperous, and how much can rulers actually cause prosperity?
- **Objectives:** analyze growth, institutions, geography, technology, and historical divergence; compare institutional, geographic, and cultural explanations of prosperity.
- **Depends on:** Unit 5, Unit 7
- **Clusters:** institutions-vs-geography-vs-culture, growth-and-state-capacity
- **Eras:** industrial state, contemporary state
- **Cases:** Britain, Botswana, Singapore, developmental states
- **Why this matters:** Prosperity is the resource base under everything else; without it, coercion and administration become much harder to sustain.

### Unit 12 — Decline and Fall — and How Much of This Is Knowable
- **Core problem:** How should rulers and scholars think about decline, collapse, and historical causation?
- **Objectives:** compare explanations of collapse and decline; analyze hindsight, survivorship bias, and the limits of causal inference; distinguish evidence, interpretation, and projection.
- **Depends on:** Unit 8, Unit 10, Unit 11
- **Clusters:** collapse-comparisons, hindsight-and-selection-bias, causal-inference
- **Eras:** all eras
- **Cases:** Rome, Qing China, the Ottoman Empire, the USSR
- **Why this matters:** The capstone unit trains the student to think historically without becoming careless, anachronistic, or overconfident.

---

## Undergraduate-level outcomes

By the end of the sequence, the student should be able to:
- explain how states form, survive, and fail;
- evaluate the interaction between coercion, legitimacy, administration, and fiscal capacity;
- compare theories from political science and history without treating them as interchangeable;
- analyze a historical case with attention to evidence, causation, and the limits of inference;
- think like a ruler in a constrained environment rather than as a spectator of politics.

---

## Build order for era and case pages

The early build order should prioritize the following materials because they are foundational across the course:
- 5 era pages: chiefdom, agrarian empire, composite monarchy, industrial nation-state, contemporary state.
- 8 case pages: Achaemenid Persia, Rome, Han China, the Abbasid caliphate, the Mongol succession crisis, Venice, the Ottoman Empire, and the USSR.
- foundational concept pages: sovereignty, state capacity, legitimacy, coercion, patronage, bureaucracy, taxation, and constitutionalism.

These should exist early because Units 1–4 depend on them immediately, and Tier 3 becomes much less useful without them.
