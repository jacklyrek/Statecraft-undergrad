# State Capacity vs. Elite Bargaining

## Question

How much of a state's durability depends on state capacity itself, and how much on the ruler's ability to keep elites aligned?

This is one of the most practical questions in statecraft because it determines where a ruler should focus attention first. If the bottleneck is administrative and fiscal, then the ruler should invest in bureaucratic reach, tax systems, and military coordination. If the bottleneck is political, then the ruler should spend more time buying loyalty, managing factions, and controlling patronage. In real life, the two are entangled. A weak state can be made more durable by a better coalition, and a strong coalition can collapse if the state cannot finance itself.

## Position 1 — State capacity is primary

The strongest version of this argument holds that durable states depend on administrative reach, fiscal systems, and coercive infrastructure. If a ruler cannot extract revenue or coordinate local officials, elite bargains do not save him for long. In this view, a coalition without institutions is fragile because it cannot convert support into durable rule. Capacity matters because taxation, logistics, and policing are what make policy real. Without them, the ruler has only promises and temporary compliance. This position is especially persuasive when the state must wage war, maintain roads and communications, or sustain a large army over time.

## Position 2 — Elite bargaining is primary

A strong rival position argues that the decisive variable is the ruler's ability to keep the coalition loyal. In many cases, state capacity is a downstream effect of elite bargains rather than a prior cause. This argument is especially powerful in settings where the state is thin but the ruler has a network of personal relationships, patronage mechanisms, or coercive ties that can hold the system together. In such settings, institutions may look weak because they depend on a coalition that is not yet fully institutionalized. This position often seems strongest in dynastic polities, frontier regimes, or early states where personal loyalty remains central.

## Best objection to each position

- The state-capacity position can understate how much military loyalty and elite cooperation are prerequisites for building capacity in the first place.
- The elite-bargaining position can understate how difficult it is to sustain a coalition when taxation, logistics, and coercion are weak.

## What evidence would discriminate

The best evidence would compare cases where rulers had similar coalitions but different administrative capacity, and cases where they had similar capacity but different elite alignment. The question is not which side is universally right, but when each mechanism dominates. A ruler needs to know whether the immediate danger is administrative failure or political fragmentation. The answer often changes with scale, era, and military technology. That is why the debate is more useful than a simple settled answer: it forces the ruler to diagnose the bottleneck rather than assume it.

## Settled vs. interpretation

The broad claim that both matter is well supported. The specific weight of each in a given case is often contested and should not be treated as settled. This is exactly why the debate is so useful. It forces the ruler to look beyond slogans and ask which constraint is binding in this case. A state may look strong on paper while being politically brittle. Or it may look weak institutionally while being surprisingly resilient because the coalition holds together.

## What this means for the person in charge

A ruler should not assume that building more capacity automatically solves coalition problems, nor that a good coalition substitutes for revenue and administration. The practical task is to know which bottleneck matters most in the case at hand. Sometimes the crisis is mainly political: an elite coalition is fraying. Sometimes it is mainly institutional: the state cannot pay, move, or command. Sometimes it is both, and the ruler must sequence interventions carefully. The point is to avoid mistaking the symptoms of weakness for the underlying cause.
