# Who Actually Keeps You in Power

The core mistake of many would-be rulers is to imagine that once power is won, the main task is to govern according to principle. In reality, the immediate task is often more elemental: preserve the coalition that made the regime possible. A ruler may hold a title, succeed in a palace coup, or win a war and still be vulnerable if the people who mattered to the victory decide they have better options. Political survival is therefore not just a matter of personal will. It is a distribution problem. Power depends on how rewards, threats, and access are distributed among the people who can make or break the regime.

Coalition politics is the art of keeping the relevant actors attached to the order without letting the order become so dependent on personal favors that it collapses under the slightest strain. A ruler who understands this can govern with a mix of incentives and coercion. A ruler who does not will constantly confront the same pattern: short-term repression creates long-term instability, and over-generous patronage weakens the state's ability to function.

## The coalition problem

Every regime is held together by some combination of coercion, legitimacy, and material reward. But the specific balance varies enormously. A small coalition can be easier to manage because the ruler can distribute benefits directly and monitor loyalty closely. A large coalition is harder because the ruler has to rely on institutions, media, or broad-based payment systems to sustain support. The difference matters because the scale of the coalition determines what kinds of strategies are feasible.

In a small coalition, the ruler can often manage loyalty through direct bargaining. In a larger coalition, the ruler must work through agents, parties, ministries, or patronage networks that can become semi-autonomous. That creates both opportunity and danger. The ruler can achieve broader base support, but the larger the coalition, the more the state is exposed to principal-agent problems and internal bargaining.

## Why patronage is not a side issue

Patronage is often treated as a dirty but secondary instrument. In practice, it is one of the central mechanisms by which political order is maintained. Patronage distributes access to offices, revenue, protection, and prestige. It can be used to buy loyalty, reward service, or punish dissent. The danger is that when patronage becomes the dominant logic of rule, the state becomes an arena for bargaining rather than administration. People begin to see office not as a public duty but as a private reward.

That is why a ruler must distinguish between patronage that strengthens institutions and patronage that merely turns the state into a market for loyalty. The former can be used to build a durable order. The latter creates a regime that survives only as long as the ruler can keep buying support.

## Why elite bargains matter

At the level of elites, the central problem is not abstract ideology but bargaining. Military leaders, provincial governors, court factions, commercial magnates, and religious authorities all ask the same question: what do I gain by supporting this ruler, and what do I lose by defecting? The ruler responds by offering a mix of office, privilege, protection, and sometimes selective punishment. These bargains are often the invisible constitution of a regime.

The ruler who ignores elite bargains often assumes that formal institutions will substitute for them. They rarely do. Formal institutions may create a structure of authority, but the actual willingness to comply depends on how elites judge their position under the new order. That is why state-building and coalition-building can look similar from the outside while being very different in practice.

## What rulers get wrong

One common mistake is to imagine that a military victory automatically produces a loyal coalition. In many cases, it creates a coalition of convenience rather than durable support. Another mistake is to think that repression alone can substitute for loyalty. Repression can work for a time, but it is costly and often produces more fragmentation in the long run. A third mistake is to treat all supporters as equally valuable. Some are essential to coercion; others are essential to legitimacy; still others are essential to revenue. The ruler must know which actors matter most in a given setting.

## Across eras

In a pre-state chiefdom, coalitions are often kin-based and personal. In an agrarian empire, loyalty is mediated by provincial elites, commanders, and local notables. In an early-modern composite monarchy, the ruler must navigate dynastic, military, and regional interests. In an industrial nation-state, coalition politics becomes more formalized and mass-based, though patronage remains central. In a contemporary state, coalitions are often organized through parties, bureaucracies, and economic networks, but the underlying problem is unchanged: the ruler must secure enough support to survive.

The invariant across eras is that rulers survive by keeping a coalition loyal. What changes is the form of the coalition and the instruments used to sustain it.
