# Unit 1 research notes

## What this unit is really about

Unit 1 is not mainly about abstract definitions of the state. It is about learning how to diagnose a political order before attempting to govern it. The core questions are practical and comparative: who can coerce, who can extract, who can coordinate, and how much of the regime's authority is actually enforceable? The best sources for this unit help the student move from slogans about sovereignty to a more grounded understanding of political capacity.

## Core sources

- Charles Tilly, “Coercion, Capital, and European States, AD 990–1990.” This is the foundational argument for linking state formation to war-making, extraction, and organizational capacity. It is especially useful for thinking about how rulers build durable polities through coercive and fiscal mechanisms rather than through legal forms alone.
- Michael Mann, The Sources of Social Power. Mann is valuable for distinguishing infrastructural power from despotic power and for clarifying the difference between formal authority and effective reach.
- Max Weber, Economy and Society. Weber remains essential for understanding domination, administrative organization, and the relationship between legitimacy and effective rule.
- James C. Scott, Seeing Like a State. Scott is useful for the administrative limits of central control and the practical costs of trying to make a state legible.
- Stephen Skowronek, The Politics Presidents Make. This is not a state-formation classic in the same way as Tilly or Weber, but it is useful for sharpening the distinction between formal authority and governing capacity.

## Secondary and comparative works

- Jack Goldstone, Revolution and Rebellion in the Early Modern World. Useful for showing how states and social mobilization interact under stress and why administrative weakness can become political crisis.
- Douglass North, John Wallis, and Barry Weingast, Violence and Social Orders. Helpful for thinking about the relationship between violence, institutions, and order across historical contexts.
- Peter Turchin, Historical Dynamics, for thinking about how state formation, elite competition, and social mobilization interact over time.

## How to read these sources

Read them as a set of lenses rather than as a single theory. Tilly is especially good on the connection between coercion and extraction. Weber is more useful for the problem of authority and legitimacy. Mann is strong on the distinction between different forms of power. Scott is useful for showing that the state often fails because it cannot actually see and coordinate the society it claims to rule. The point is not to choose one master theory but to build a practical toolkit for analyzing states under pressure.

## Questions to carry into the unit

- What institutions actually enforce decisions?
- Which actors can block policy, and why?
- Is the ruler's problem mainly military, fiscal, administrative, or political?
- How much of the state's apparent order depends on coercion rather than consent?
- How does the answer change across eras and scales?

## Research use

Read these sources as tools for diagnosing political order under pressure. Unit 1 is less about discovering a single theory of the state than about developing a practical set of questions: who can coerce, who can extract, who can coordinate, and how durable is the regime when those capabilities are weak? The most productive way to use the unit is to compare the theories against concrete cases rather than to memorize them in isolation.
