# Statecraft: An Undergraduate-Level Self-Study Syllabus for Rulers and States

*Designed for someone who wants to study political order at the level of an undergraduate major in political science, history, or international relations: not as a list of quotations, but as an intellectually serious introduction to the practical problems of rule, state formation, governance, and historical change.*

---

## How to use this syllabus

This course is organized around a simple but demanding premise: to rule well, one must understand how states actually work, how regimes survive, and how historical institutions change over time. The units are sequenced in the order in which governing problems typically arise: first establish control, then sustain support, then build administrative and fiscal capacity, then manage crisis, war, and decline. The sequence is not merely topical. It is meant to train a ruler's-eye way of thinking: what is the bottleneck, what is the trade-off, and what kind of evidence would change the judgment?

Each unit is designed to do three things at once:
- teach a core concept or family of concepts,
- connect that concept to historical cases and cross-era comparison,
- and train the student to think about policy, governance, and strategic choice under constraint.

The course assumes the student is reading actively, comparing interpretations, and studying not only what happened but why historians and political scientists disagree about it.

# TIER 1 — Taking and Keeping Power

## Unit 1: What You Are Actually Seizing

**Objectives**
- Distinguish state, regime, government, and political order.
- Analyze sovereignty, monopoly on violence, state formation, and state capacity as practical problems of rule.
- Explain why state formation is not simply a legal event but an organizational and coercive one.
- Compare how these problems appear across pre-state, agrarian, early-modern, industrial, and contemporary forms.

**Core concepts**
- State formation and war-making; Tilly and his critics.
- Sovereignty as both claim and capability.
- The monopoly on violence as a practical requirement of rule.
- State capacity as the ability to convert authority into reliable administration.
- Fragility, uneven enforcement, and the gap between formal institutions and real power.

**Methodological emphasis**
- Distinguish causal claims from descriptive claims.
- Learn to evaluate how historians and political scientists explain state-building differently.
- Practice comparing institutional capacity across eras rather than assuming modern institutions are universal.

**Anchor resources**
- Tilly, *Coercion, Capital, and European States*.
- Mann, *The Sources of Social Power*.
- Weber, *Economy and Society*.

**Why it matters in the chair**
- If the ruler cannot command the apparatus, then policy is theater. The first task of rule is to make the state actually work.

**Practice rep**
- Write a diagnostic memo on the first five institutions you would inspect after seizing power in a weak or fragmented polity.

---

## Unit 2: Who Actually Keeps You in Power

**Objectives**
- Explain why rulers depend on coalitions rather than personal charisma alone.
- Distinguish winning coalitions, loyal coalitions, and patronage networks.
- Analyze how coalition size changes incentives, information problems, and the cost of governance.
- Evaluate the relationship between material reward, legitimacy, and coercion.

**Core concepts**
- Selectorate theory and winning coalitions.
- Patronage, brokerage, elite bargaining, and the logic of access.
- Factionalism, loyalty, and the politics of reward distribution.
- The public-goods problem in rule.
- The distinction between governing a small coalition and governing a mass polity.

**Methodological emphasis**
- Compare formal models with historical case evidence.
- Study how power-sharing and patronage can stabilize or destabilize a regime.
- Learn to identify when a ruler is managing a coalition and when he is merely managing appearances.

**Anchor resources**
- Bueno de Mesquita & Smith, *The Dictator's Handbook*.
- Machiavelli, *The Prince*.
- Ibn Khaldun, *The Muqaddimah*.

**Why it matters in the chair**
- A ruler survives by keeping the coalition loyal, not by being loved in the abstract.

**Practice rep**
- Compare how a ruler might distribute resources in a 50-person coalition versus a 50-million-person one, and identify the strategic trade-offs in each case.

---

## Unit 3: Legitimacy and Succession

**Objectives**
- Explain why people obey when they do not have to.
- Distinguish legitimacy from mere obedience or fear.
- Analyze how regimes produce legitimacy through ritual, law, performance, religion, and institutions.
- Evaluate succession as a regime-level vulnerability and a test of institutional resilience.

**Core concepts**
- Weber's three ideal types of legitimacy: traditional, charismatic, and rational-legal.
- Ritual, ceremony, and spectacle as instruments of rule.
- Information control and symbolic politics.
- Succession crises as a major source of regime breakdown.
- The difference between legitimacy in theory and legitimacy under stress.

**Methodological emphasis**
- Compare normative and empirical approaches to legitimacy.
- Study how legitimacy is produced and contested in different historical settings.
- Evaluate why legitimacy sometimes matters most precisely when the regime is weakest.

**Anchor resources**
- Weber, *Economy and Society*.
- Beetham, *The Legitimation of Power*.
- Goldstone, *Revolution and Rebellion in the Early Modern World*.

**Why it matters in the chair**
- The most reliable way to kill a regime may be to make power transfer seem uncertain or illegitimate.

**Practice rep**
- Draft a legitimacy strategy for a ruler inheriting a realm with weak institutions and a contested succession.

---

## Unit 4: The Means of Coercion

**Objectives**
- Analyze how armies, police, militias, guards, and mercenaries shape political order.
- Understand the coup-proofing dilemma.
- Compare coercive systems across eras and explain how technology and social structure affect them.
- Evaluate the political dangers of militarized rule.

**Core concepts**
- Praetorians, mercenaries, conscript armies, standing armies, and secret police.
- The relation between coercive capacity and political control.
- The permanent dilemma: the force that protects the ruler can also remove him.
- Military organization as both instrument and political actor.

**Methodological emphasis**
- Compare military history with political science on the role of coercion.
- Study how military design affects regime durability.
- Distinguish between capability and loyalty.

**Anchor resources**
- Tilly, *War Making and State Making as Organized Crime*.
- Huntington, *Political Order in Changing Societies*.
- Mann, *The Sources of Social Power*.

**Why it matters in the chair**
- The army is at once the ruler's shield and the ruler's greatest internal threat.

**Practice rep**
- Write the strongest objection to a coercive design you believe would be effective in a fragile regime.

---

# TIER 2 — Making the Machine Work

## Unit 5: Money and Fiscal Capacity

**Objectives**
- Analyze taxation, revenue extraction, and fiscal capacity across historical contexts.
- Explain why states fail when they cannot finance themselves.
- Compare tribute, plunder, land tax, customs, income tax, debt, and inflation as instruments of rule.
- Evaluate how fiscal systems shape the state and the regime.

**Core concepts**
- Taxation and public finance.
- Coinage, debasement, inflation, and sovereign debt.
- The fiscal-military state.
- Fiscal fragility and the political costs of weak revenue systems.

**Methodological emphasis**
- Study the relationship between economic structure and political organization.
- Compare state finance across premodern and modern settings.
- Assess how fiscal capacity changes war-making and administrative reach.

**Anchor resources**
- Scheve & Stasavage, *Taxing the Rich*.
- North, Wallis & Weingast, *Violence and Social Orders*.
- Stasavage, *States of Credit*.

**Why it matters in the chair**
- A ruler who cannot finance the state eventually loses the ability to govern.

**Practice rep**
- Compare the fiscal trade-offs of tax farming, tribute extraction, and salaried bureaucracy in a premodern empire.

---

## Unit 6: Administration and Information Distortion

**Objectives**
- Understand bureaucracy as a mechanism of rule and as a source of distortion.
- Analyze principal-agent problems, corruption, and information asymmetry.
- Explain why orders often arrive distorted over distance.
- Evaluate what the filing cabinet, telegraph, census, and digital record have changed in statecraft.

**Core concepts**
- Bureaucracy and rule by paperwork.
- Corruption, legibility, administrative reach, and the politics of implementation.
- The Chinese examination system and the modern civil service.
- Information technology and the compression of distance.

**Methodological emphasis**
- Study how bureaucratic systems affect both policy outcomes and political trust.
- Learn to distinguish implementation failure from strategic resistance.
- Compare different forms of administrative legibility.

**Anchor resources**
- Scott, *Seeing Like a State*.
- Weber, *Economy and Society*.
- Evans, *Embedded Autonomy*.

**Why it matters in the chair**
- The hard part of ruling is often making orders survive the chain of command without being warped by incentives and local power.

**Practice rep**
- Identify three ways information distortion could break an administrative reform in an agrarian empire.

---

## Unit 7: Binding Yourself

**Objectives**
- Understand rule of law as credible commitment.
- Analyze how constitutions, courts, property rights, and public institutions help rulers borrow and survive.
- Compare constitutional forms and their implications for authority and accountability.

**Core concepts**
- Rule of law and credible commitment.
- Courts, property rights, contracts, and enforcement.
- Constitutions and institutional constraints.
- Presidential, parliamentary, and semi-presidential systems.
- The trade-off between flexibility and durability.

**Methodological emphasis**
- Compare constitutional design with historical institutionalism.
- Think about the difference between legal form and effective enforcement.
- Study how institutions shape incentives without eliminating politics.

**Anchor resources**
- North, Wallis & Weingast, *Violence and Social Orders*.
- Acemoglu & Robinson, *Why Nations Fail*.
- constitutional history and case studies.

**Why it matters in the chair**
- The ruler who binds himself sometimes survives longer than the ruler who does not.

**Practice rep**
- Draft a constitutional bargain that would increase a ruler's credibility without destroying their power to govern.

---

## Unit 8: Deciding Under Pressure

**Objectives**
- Understand how advisors, institutions, and information shape crisis decision-making.
- Analyze how leaders actually control events and how much they really control.
- Evaluate the difference between structure and agency under pressure.

**Core concepts**
- Allison's three models of foreign policy decision-making.
- Groupthink, advisory design, and bounded rationality.
- The autocrat's information problem.
- The limits of central command in crises.

**Methodological emphasis**
- Compare decision-making models with case evidence.
- Study how uncertainty and organizational incentives distort policy.
- Learn to separate poor judgment from poor information.

**Anchor resources**
- Allison, *Essence of Decision*.
- Janis, *Groupthink*.
- case-based work on crisis management.

**Why it matters in the chair**
- In the real world, many failures are not the result of a bad plan but of poor information and poor coordination.

**Practice rep**
- Write a decision memo for a ruler facing a sudden war scare with weak information.

---

# TIER 3 — Surviving History

## Unit 9: Neighbors and Grand Strategy

**Objectives**
- Understand anarchy, the security dilemma, alliances, deterrence, and trade.
- Analyze how grand strategy is shaped by domestic constraints.
- Explain war as both a policy instrument and a budget problem.
- Compare different theoretical approaches to international politics.

**Core concepts**
- Realism, liberalism, and constructivism.
- Balance of power, alliance politics, and hegemony.
- Deterrence and nuclear strategy.
- Power transition and the politics of decline.

**Methodological emphasis**
- Learn to connect domestic political constraints to foreign policy choices.
- Compare systemic explanations with state-level ones.
- Evaluate how war shapes the fiscal and political capacities of the state.

**Anchor resources**
- Waltz, *Theory of International Politics*.
- Mearsheimer, *The Tragedy of Great Power Politics*.
- strategic case studies.

**Why it matters in the chair**
- Foreign policy is constrained by the same revenue, coalition, and legitimacy problems that dominate domestic politics.

**Practice rep**
- Compare the strategic challenges facing a rising power and a declining hegemon.

---

## Unit 10: Rupture from Within

**Objectives**
- Understand revolution, civil war, insurgency, and democratization.
- Explain how rulers either invite or forestall rupture.
- Connect domestic legitimacy, inequality, repression, and social mobilization to the risk of rebellion.

**Core concepts**
- Revolution and state breakdown.
- Civil war onset and insurgency.
- Ethnic conflict, peasant rebellion, and mass mobilization.
- Democratization, backsliding, and authoritarian resilience.

**Methodological emphasis**
- Compare structural causes with contingency and agency.
- Study the difference between grievances and mobilization.
- Analyze the role of repression, information, and coalition collapse in regime breakdown.

**Anchor resources**
- Skocpol, *States and Social Revolutions*.
- Goldstone, *Revolution and Rebellion in the Early Modern World*.
- Fearon & Laitin, “Ethnicity, Insurgency, and Civil War.”

**Why it matters in the chair**
- Internal rupture is often the collapse of the coalitions and legitimacy that made rule possible.

**Practice rep**
- Identify one policy that would reduce the risk of revolution and one that would increase it, and defend each judgment.

---

## Unit 11: Prosperity and the Resource Base

**Objectives**
- Understand long-run growth and its relation to state capacity.
- Distinguish institutions, geography, culture, and technology as explanations for prosperity.
- Evaluate how rulers can sometimes cause prosperity but also often only avoid destroying it.

**Core concepts**
- Industrialization and the Great Divergence.
- Institutions vs. geography vs. culture.
- State capacity, economic organization, and the politics of growth.
- The ruler's version of growth: can I cause prosperity, or only avoid destroying the conditions that support it?

**Methodological emphasis**
- Compare competing explanations of development and underdevelopment.
- Study when institutions are causal and when they mainly mediate other forces.
- Evaluate the role of colonialism, trade, and technology in state-building.

**Anchor resources**
- Acemoglu & Robinson, *Why Nations Fail*.
- Pomeranz, *The Great Divergence*.
- Mokyr, *A Culture of Growth*.

**Why it matters in the chair**
- Prosperity is the resource base under everything else. Without it, coercion, legitimacy, and administration become much harder to sustain.

**Practice rep**
- Write the strongest case that institutions matter more than geography for growth in the modern era, then identify the strongest criticism of that claim.

---

## Unit 12: Decline and Fall — and How Much of This Is Knowable

**Objectives**
- Compare explanations of decline and collapse.
- Apply methods for evaluating hard historical claims.
- Recognize the methodological traps of hindsight, survivorship bias, and retrospective narrative.
- Distinguish between evidence, interpretation, and projection.

**Core concepts**
- Fiscal-military exhaustion, complexity, and diminishing returns.
- Environmental shock, epidemic, and imperial overstretch.
- The methodological case that collapse is partly a product of surviving sources and later narratives.
- Counterfactuals and disciplined causal inference.

**Methodological emphasis**
- Learn how to evaluate whether a claim about collapse is well supported or merely plausible.
- Practice distinguishing between strong historical explanation and retrospective storytelling.
- Study the difference between a case and a law.

**Anchor resources**
- Tainter, *The Collapse of Complex Societies*.
- Ibn Khaldun, *The Muqaddimah*.
- Gibbon, *The History of the Decline and Fall of the Roman Empire*.

**Why it matters in the chair**
- This unit is the capstone because it asks what can actually be known about the rise and fall of regimes, and how to avoid turning history into a morality tale.

**Practice rep**
- Write one explanation for the collapse of a state that you would defend and one that you would reject, with the evidence and reasoning for each.

---

## Undergraduate-level outcomes

By the end of the course, the student should be able to:
- explain how states form, survive, and fail;
- evaluate the interaction between coercion, legitimacy, administration, and fiscal capacity;
- compare theories from political science and history without treating them as interchangeable;
- analyze a historical case with attention to evidence, causation, and the limits of inference;
- think like a ruler in a constrained environment rather than like a spectator of politics.

---

## Build order for era and case pages

The early build order should prioritize:
- 5 era pages: chiefdom, agrarian empire, composite monarchy, industrial nation-state, contemporary state.
- 8 case pages: Achaemenid Persia, Rome, Han China, the Abbasid caliphate, the Mongol succession crisis, Venice, the Ottoman Empire, and the USSR.

These should exist early because Units 1–4 depend on them immediately, and Tier 3 becomes much less useful without them. The course should also build a small set of foundational concept pages early: sovereignty, state capacity, legitimacy, coercion, patronage, and bureaucracy, since those concepts recur across the whole syllabus.
